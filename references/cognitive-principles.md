## Original
Primary words, where I opened the source:
- Miller: "the span of absolute judgment and the span of immediate memory are quite different kinds of limitations that are imposed on our ability to process information."
- Miller: "Absolute judgment is limited by the amount of information. Immediate memory is limited by the number of items."
- Nielsen (Jakob's law): "Users spend most of their time on *other* sites."
- Postel: "be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept from others."

Yablonski's one-line forms (lawsofux.com), word for word. These are Yablonski's words, not the originators':
- Fitts's law: "The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target."
- Hick's law: "The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices."
- Serial position effect: "Users have a propensity to best remember the first and last items in a series."
- Von Restorff effect: "When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered."
- Peak-end rule: "People judge an experience largely based on how they felt at its peak and at its end, rather than the total sum or average of every moment of the experience."
- Doherty threshold: "Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures that neither has to wait on the other."
- Tesler's law: "For any system there is a certain amount of complexity which cannot be reduced."

## For and not for
Our summary, by grade. "Holds when" is the condition of the original study, as we read it.

| Principle | Grade | Holds when | Not for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fitts's law | Solid | A person points at a target; time grows with distance and falls with size | Keyboard paths; reading time |
| Hick-Hyman law | Solid | Simple choice reaction time: known, practised stimulus–response pairs | Search of an unfamiliar or long list (that is a scan); a claim that fewer options is always better |
| Serial position | Narrow | Recall of a list from memory | Choice from a visible list |
| Von Restorff | Narrow | Recall of one item that differs from the rest of a list | Proof that a highlight draws action |
| Peak-end | Narrow | A remembered judgement of a whole episode | Usability in the moment |
| Doherty threshold | Narrow | System response time in repeated transaction work | A fixed rule for every wait |
| Jakob's law | Maxim | A person brings conventions from other products | Proof; cite as a maxim only |
| Tesler's law | Maxim | Some complexity stays; the design picks who carries it | Proof; cite as a maxim only |
| Postel's law | Maxim | Input that the system can accept in more than one form | Proof; cite as a maxim only |
| Miller's 7±2 | Not used | Span of absolute judgement and immediate memory | Never for menu or item counts. A visible menu needs no recall. |

## Our operationalisation
- Cite a principle only when a named decision depends on it.
- Solid principles can support a finding alone. Narrow principles need their condition met in the artifact. Say how it is met.
- Cite a maxim as a maxim, never as proof. A maxim alone never sets a severity above MINOR.
- Never cite Miller's 7±2 for menu or item counts.
- Dimensions this lens covers: cognitive load, task efficiency.

Cite the principle by name and number. State the evidence in the artifact. A principle with no bearing on a named decision is not cited.

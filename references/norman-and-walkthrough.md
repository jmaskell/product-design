# Norman and the cognitive walkthrough

## Source
- Norman, D. A. *The Design of Everyday Things*. Revised and expanded edition. Basic Books, 2013.
- Norman, D. A. "Signifiers, not affordances". *ACM Interactions* 15/6, 2008. Confirmed by fetching https://jnd.org/signifiers-not-affordances/ (2026-10-07).
- Wharton, C., Rieman, J., Lewis, C. and Polson, P. "The cognitive walkthrough method: a practitioner's guide". In Nielsen, J. and Mack, R. L. (eds.), *Usability Inspection Methods*. John Wiley and Sons, 1994.

Unverified: I did not open the book or the 1994 chapter. The concept names below are Norman's. Their one-line glosses are our summary, not his words.

## Original
Norman, "Signifiers, not affordances", word for word:
- Affordance: "The term was originally invented by the perceptual psychologist J. J. Gibson to refer to a relationship: the actions possible by a specific agent on a specific environment."
- Signifier: "A 'signifier' is some sort of indicator, some signal in the physical or social world that can be interpreted meaningfully."
- "The perceivable part of an affordance is a signifier, and if deliberately placed by a designer, it is a social signifier."
- "Designers of the world: Forget affordances provide signifiers."

Norman's concepts from *The Design of Everyday Things* (names only; glosses are our summary):
| Concept | Our summary |
|---|---|
| Affordance vs signifier | What a person can do, against what tells them they can do it |
| Mapping | The relation between a control and its effect |
| Constraints | Limits that make the wrong action hard or impossible |
| Feedback | The result of an action, shown at once |
| Conceptual model | The person's explanation of how the thing works |
| Gulf of execution | The gap between what the person wants and what they can see to do |
| Gulf of evaluation | The gap between what happened and what the person can tell happened |
| Seven stages of action | Goal; plan, specify, perform (execution); perceive, interpret, compare (evaluation) |

Unverified: the four walkthrough questions in the 1994 wording. Secondary sources give this form:
1. Will the user try to achieve the right effect?
2. Will the user notice that the correct action is available?
3. Will the user associate the correct action with the effect that user is trying to achieve?
4. If the correct action is performed, will the user see that progress is being made toward solution of the task?

## For and not for
Our summary: the walkthrough is for learnability. It asks whether a first-time person can find and complete each step with no training.
Our summary: not for expert speed. A design can pass every walkthrough step and still slow a daily expert. Pair it with a usability or cognitive lens for that.

## Our operationalisation
- Walk the task flow in journey.md one step at a time, as the named actor.
- At each step, ask the four questions. A "no" is a finding at that step.
- Name the gulf: execution (the person cannot see what to do) or evaluation (the person cannot tell what happened).
- Name the Norman concept that fails: signifier, mapping, constraint, feedback or conceptual model.
- Walk once as a first-time person and once as the most frequent actor. Report where the two walks disagree.
- Dimensions this lens covers: task efficiency, mental-model fit, information clarity, user control, error prevention, error recovery, evidence confidence.

Cite the principle by name and number. State the evidence in the artifact. A principle with no bearing on a named decision is not cited.

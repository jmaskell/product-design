# Service blueprinting

## Source
- Shostack, G. L. "Designing Services That Deliver". *Harvard Business Review* 62/1, January–February 1984, pp. 133–139. Confirmed by fetching https://hbr.org/1984/01/designing-services-that-deliver (title, author, date) and by citation note 9 in Bitner et al. (volume, pages).
- Bitner, M. J., Ostrom, A. L. and Morgan, F. N. "Service Blueprinting: A Practical Technique for Service Innovation". *California Management Review* 50/3, Spring 2008, pp. 66–94. Confirmed by reading the full text (reprint PDF found by search, 2026-10-07).
- Gibbons, S. "Service Blueprints: Definition". Nielsen Norman Group, 27 August 2017. Confirmed by fetching https://www.nngroup.com/articles/service-blueprints-definition/ (2026-10-07).

Unverified: Shostack's own words. The HBR page showed only the opening paragraph. Bitner et al. state that blueprinting began as "a process control technique for services" that "was able to identify failure points in a service operation", and they credit Shostack.

## Original elements
Bitner et al.: "There are five components of a typical service blueprint":
| Component | Bitner et al. words |
|---|---|
| Customer actions | "all of the steps that customers take as part of the service delivery process." |
| Onstage/visible contact employee actions | "separated from the customer by the line of interaction." |
| Backstage/invisible contact employee actions | "separated from the onstage actions by the very important line of visibility." |
| Support processes | "all of the activities carried out by individuals and units within the company who are not contact employees but that need to happen in order for the service to be delivered." |
| Physical evidence | Listed as the fifth component. |

The lines:
- Line of interaction. Bitner et al.: "Every time the line of interaction is crossed via a link from the customer to a contact employee (or company self-service technology), a moment of truth has occurred."
- Line of visibility. Bitner et al.: "Everything that appears above the line of visibility is seen by the customer, while everything below it is invisible."
- Line of internal interaction. NN/g: "Separates contact employees from those who do not directly support interactions with customers/users." Bitner et al. call it the "internal line of interaction".

Bitner et al. on order: "the actions of the customer are central to the creation of the blueprint, and as such they are typically laid out first".
Bitner et al. on self-service: remove the onstage employee row and "replace it with an onstage technology row".
NN/g on journey maps: "Think of service blueprints as a part two to customer journey maps."

## For and not for
Our summary: for showing the work behind each stage, and who does it, when the person cannot see it.
Our summary: for finding hand-offs and failure points between actors.
Our summary: not for discovering needs. A blueprint does not discover needs. It shows how a service delivers a journey that research already found.

## Our operationalisation
- One journey per outcome. Other actors appear as rows when they act in a stage.
- Three levels, always in this order: experience journey (the whole outcome, also outside the product) → task flow (steps and decisions for one goal) → interface flow (screens; designing-interactions owns it).
- Every cell that states behaviour carries an evidence tag.
- Add the Backstage section only when a stage has a hand-off or work the person cannot see.
- In the Backstage table, "Visible to <actor>?" records the line of visibility for that row. A "no" there plus a new action is work the change moves out of sight. Write it in the displacement table.
